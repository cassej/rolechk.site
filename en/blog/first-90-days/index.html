First 90 Days in One Dimension: How Rick Learns to Trust Someone Else at the Wheel

So, Rick, you did it. You hired an Integrator. A real, system-minded guy with steel balls who isn't afraid of your mental raids. You gave him the keys to the garage, sat in the passenger seat of the flying saucer, and got ready to watch your business transform into an intergalactic corporation.

But by the end of the first week, your eye is already twitching.

Your new Morty blocked direct access to the developers, canceled your "brilliant" Friday promotion because it breaks the margins, and made you (!) fill out a report by Monday. Your ego is screaming like a Cronenberg stuck in the textures: "It's my business! I built it from scratch when you weren't even in the project! Give me the wheel, I'll redo everything myself!"

Easy, Rick. Put down the portal gun. What you're feeling right now is called the Adaptation Crisis in Rocket Fuel. This is the most dangerous period, when 80% of Visionaries crack, fire their awesome Integrator, and crawl back into their familiar, cozy operational hell.

Let's break down how to survive the first 90 days without getting a divorce.

Phase 1 Days 1–30: Inventory of Chaos

Your Morty's first month in the company is not a time for grand achievements. It's when he looks under every rug in your garage and quietly freaks out at what he sees. He studies the P&L (if it even exists), digs through the crooked CRM, talks to the team, and tries to understand why the processes are held together with duct tape and spit.

Your job in this period: Shut up and don't interfere. It will seem like he's slow, that he's "not dynamic enough." Remember: to build a system, you first have to study the old foundation. Your only duty right now is to honestly answer his questions and give him access to the numbers.

Phase 2 Days 31–60: Withdrawal and Crisis of Control

Now the real meat show begins. In the second month, the Integrator starts implementing the Accountability Chart and cutting the fat. He removes duplicate functions, introduces hard metrics for departments, and — the most painful part — starts filtering your ideas.

You come running in screaming: "I figured out how to sell our software to reptiloids from dimension 35C!" and he coolly blocks the task because the team hasn't even finished building the funnel for Earthlings.

You'll go through hard withdrawal. It will feel like the system is "suffocating" your creativity.

How to survive: Understand that systemization is not a prison for your genius. It's rails. Without those rails, your train will just fly into a ditch. When Morty says "not now," it means "not now," not "your idea sucks." Learn to trust his operational intuition the same way he trusts your strategic vision.

Phase 3 Days 61–90: First Fruits and the United Front Syndrome

By the third month, the fog starts to clear. The team gets used to the new rules. Employees stop running to you crying about the "tough director" because they know Rick will just send them back to Morty (you do remember the United Front Rule, right?).

You suddenly catch yourself thinking:

  • You sleep at night instead of rebuilding ad campaigns;
  • On calls, department heads speak in numbers, not excuses;
  • You have free time to actually think about next year's strategy or go to a conference.

The chaos in the garage is turning into an automated conveyor belt.

Checklist for Rick How to Know the Break-In Succeeded

By day 90, you should reach the next level of partnership:

  • You regularly hold Same Page Meetings and don't want to kill each other afterward.
  • The team clearly knows who their boss is. For all operational questions, they go to the Integrator. They come to you only for a dose of inspiration or scale.
  • You've voluntarily surrendered the final say on budget, hiring line staff, and deadlines.

Bottom line for the mad genius: Handing over the wheel of your company to another person is like giving your firstborn to be raised by an alien race. Painful, terrifying, your ego convulses. But it's the only way to turn your garage startup into an empire that outlives you.
